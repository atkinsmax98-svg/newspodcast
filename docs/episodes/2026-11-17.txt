As runners push their limits across all distances, sports science continues to examine how body mechanics and training methods shape athletic outcomes. Time Magazine recently tackled a growing trend in gym training: the manual treadmill. Unlike conventional motorized treadmills that pull the belt beneath the runner's feet, manual treadmills require the user to drive the curved belt purely through muscle exertion. Time explains that while manual treadmills engage the posterior chain—specifically the hamstrings and glutes—much more aggressively, they also demand higher metabolic energy expenditure at lower speeds. Experts advise runners transitioning to manual treadmills to cut their standard workout durations down initially, paying close attention to stride mechanics to avoid overloading lower-leg tendons.

For runners recovering from injury or attempting to reduce impact forces, a new synthesis of exercise physiology featured on RUN, powered by Outside, addresses a perennial question: can you stay race-ready without running? The review examines scientific data on non-impact cross-training modalities such as deep-water pool running, heavy-resistance cycling, and elliptical training. Researchers found that high-intensity interval training performed in the pool or on a bike can maintain VO2 max—a key measure of maximum oxygen uptake—and cardiac output for several weeks without significant losses in aerobic fitness. However, the study notes that cross-training cannot fully replicate the neuromuscular adaptations and tissue resilience created by ground impact, meaning runners must reintroduce ground contact gradually before stepping back up to the starting line.
