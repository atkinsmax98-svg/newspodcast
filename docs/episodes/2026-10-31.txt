Now, let's turn to the medical and scientific research explaining how these grueling athletic efforts affect the human body. As participation in marathons and ultramarathons continues to grow worldwide, sports cardiologists are taking a much closer look at cardiac health.

Time Magazine reports on recent findings examining what running a marathon actually does to the human heart. While regular aerobic exercise is well-established as a cornerstone of long-term cardiovascular health, researchers emphasize that the acute physical stress of running 26.2 miles causes temporary physiological changes. These include temporary heart chamber dilation, transient elevation of cardiac biomarkers like troponin, and short-term arterial stiffness. While the heart typically recovers fully within days or weeks, the findings underscore the importance of proper training and gradual mileage progression.

Building on these insights, Marathon Handbook takes a deep dive into what researchers refer to as the Athlete Paradox. While lifelong endurance runners usually exhibit low resting heart rates, high aerobic capacity, and excellent metabolic markers, advanced cardiovascular imaging reveals that some veteran endurance athletes unexpectedly show higher levels of coronary artery calcification and plaque buildup compared to moderate exercisers. Scientists are actively studying whether this exercise-induced plaque is structurally more stable and less prone to rupture than plaque caused by sedentary lifestyles, but the research highlights that intense exercise is not a complete guarantee against underlying heart disease.
